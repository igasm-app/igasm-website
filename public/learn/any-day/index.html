What holds on every day of the month, for every body: responsive desire, pain, pleasure, lube and drinks.

Written by the igasm team from the studies and health services cited on this page. Not yet reviewed by a clinician: a named reviewer and a review date will appear here before this page is listed in search.

Desire can arrive after starting

For many people desire is responsive: it shows up once touch and closeness begin, not before. Not feeling spontaneous desire is common, and is not a sign something is wrong.

Moderate evidence · Source: Basson 2000

Pain is a signal, not duty

Painful sex is more common than many people think, and it is never something to push through. Stop or change what hurts. If pain keeps coming back, a clinician or pelvic health physio can help.

Strong evidence · Source: Mitchell et al. 2017

Hands, mouths and toys count

In a national survey of people with a vulva, under 1 in 5 said intercourse alone was enough to orgasm; most said clitoral touch was needed or made it better. Hands, mouths and toys are not extras. Many show a partner what works.

Moderate evidence · Source: Herbenick et al. 2018

Time on your own counts

In a large national survey, solo masturbation was common in every adult age group. Pleasure on your own is part of a sex life, not a stand-in for one.

Strong evidence · Source: Herbenick et al. 2010

Quiet spells are common

In a national survey in Britain, many people said interest in sex had dropped away for three months or more in the past year. A quiet stretch is common, and it is not a sign something is broken.

Strong evidence · Source: Mitchell et al. 2013

Choose lube that suits you

Oil-based products weaken latex condoms. Water-based and silicone-based lubes are condom-friendly. If a product stings or burns, stop and try a gentler one.

Strong evidence · Source: Condom-lubricant study

Drinks change the body's response

Alcohol can make you feel more in the mood while lab studies show the body responds less and orgasm takes longer at higher doses. At lighter amounts the effect is smaller.

Moderate evidence · Source: George et al. 2011

More is not always better

In large studies of people in relationships, wellbeing rose with how often they had sex up to about once a week, then levelled off. Frequency is not a score. What feels right for both of you is what counts.

Moderate evidence · Source: Muise, Schimmack and Impett 2016

Something new, together

In experiments, couples who shared new and a little exciting activities felt more satisfied with their relationship afterwards. Small new things count, in or out of the bedroom.

Moderate evidence · Source: Aron et al. 2000
