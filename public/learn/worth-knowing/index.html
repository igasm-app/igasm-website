Things worth knowing

igasm, by Venex Labs. Last updated 4 November 2026.

Six well-described findings about desire and cycles, each with its source. They describe people in general, never you in particular.

Written by the igasm team from the studies and health services cited on this page. Not yet reviewed by a clinician: a named reviewer and a review date will appear here before this page is listed in search.

Desire does not always come first

For many people desire shows up once things begin rather than before, and most people experience both kinds at different times. Researchers describe this as responsive desire; it is a common way desire works.

Source: Basson model, reviewed in Brotto and Graham (2021)

Cycle effects are real, and small

Large diary studies find desire tends to rise a little around the middle of the cycle on average, but the size of that shift differs a lot from person to person, and many people show no clear pattern at all.

Source: Arslan et al. (2021), 26,000+ diary entries

Almost no cycle runs exactly 28 days

Across more than 600,000 tracked cycles the average was about 29 days, and most of the difference between cycles came from the days before the middle of the cycle. That is why igasm shows the next period as a range and calls the current phase an estimate.

Source: Bull et al. (2019), 612,613 cycles

Everyone has a monthly rhythm

Desire rises and falls over days and weeks for people of every sex, cycle or not. A rhythm in your log is not always about a cycle.

Source: Vowels et al. (2018)

Quiet days count

Rating desire only on days something happened gives a lopsided picture. Daily check-ins on quiet days too are how diary research measures desire, which is why igasm asks for them.

Source: Vowels et al. (2018); Roney and Simmons (2013)

Hormonal methods and desire vary

In a review of 36 studies, most people using a combined pill reported unchanged or higher desire, and a minority reported lower. If yours changed, a clinician is the right person to talk to; never stop a method on your own.

Source: Pastor, Holla and Chmel (2013), systematic review
