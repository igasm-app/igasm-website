How igasm decides a pattern is real

igasm, by Venex Labs. Last updated 2 November 2026.

A few logged moments always look like a pattern. Three good nights on a Friday is not a Friday effect; it is three nights. Most apps show you the pattern anyway, because a confident sentence feels like value. igasm does the opposite: it waits until your own log can support a sentence, and when it cannot, it tells you that plainly. "No clear pattern yet" is a real answer.

The short version

  • Only you. Every pattern compares you with you. There are no population averages, no "normal range" and no comparison with anyone else.
  • Small groups are pulled toward your usual. A handful of moments barely moves the picture; a lot of moments speaks for itself.
  • Enough on both sides. To say something goes with your warmer moments, igasm needs at least 8 moments with it and 8 without.
  • Big enough, and steady enough. The difference has to be meaningful after that pull, and its 90% range has to stay on one side of zero.
  • Tested on people with no pattern at all. Before any analysis ships, it runs on thousands of simulated people whose data has no real pattern in it. It is allowed to name a pattern for at most 1 in 10 of them, and it still has to find a real, clear difference most of the time.

1. Partial pooling: why three nights are not a pattern

Every group of moments (a part of your cycle, a day of the week, a kind of evening) starts at your own usual and only moves away from it as moments add up. With 3 moments a group stays close to your usual; with 30 it mostly speaks for itself. Statisticians call this partial pooling, and it is the standard way to stop small, noisy groups from producing confident claims (Gelman, Hill and Yajima, 2012).

2. Enough on both sides, spread over time

A comparison needs at least 8 moments on each side before it is even tried. Cycle patterns need at least two cycles, because one cycle cannot tell a phase from a week that happened to be good. Daily check-ins are stricter still: one day is tied to the next, so a comparison needs days spread across at least four separate weeks on each side, and the same direction in at least two separate months.

3. Meaningful, and clearly so

After the pull toward your usual, the difference has to be large enough to matter on the scale you rated, and the 90% range around it must not cross zero. If both are not true, igasm says no clear pattern yet, and tells you what more it would need, never as a target and never as a count of days to hit.

4. A false-alarm budget, measured

Looking at many things at once guarantees that some of them will look special by chance. So igasm does not trust its own thresholds; it measures them. Each analysis is run on thousands of simulated people whose data contains no real effect at all, across different amounts of data and noise. If it names a pattern for more than 1 in 10 of them, the thresholds are tightened until it does not. When one family of comparisons grew from 11 contexts to 22, this test failed at the old setting and the pull toward your usual was strengthened before it shipped.

5. Words that say only what is true

  • "In your log, X has tended to come with..." describes what has been. It never says X causes anything, and never tells you to do more of it.
  • Nothing is a prediction. igasm never tells you a best day, a best time, or when to do anything.
  • Nothing is about fertility or contraception, and nothing is a diagnosis. Cycle patterns describe your own moments, not your body's chances.
